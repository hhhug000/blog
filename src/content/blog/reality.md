People treat simulation theory like a scifi gimmick, but it is actually a useful way to test what we mean when we call something "real". Most people assume that if a world is artificial or simulated, it does not matter. If our universe turned out to be a computer program rather than nature, Most people think that our existence would lose its value. But that is a flawed definition of reality. Realness is not about whether something is made of raw physical stuff and atoms. It is about whether it has actual consequences.

To see why an artificial world would still be real, you have to look at how much of our lives already relies on things that are not physical. We interact with things every day that have no physical mass. Stuff like debt, laws and rules. None of these exist in nature. You cannot dig up a law out of the ground. They only exist in our minds. Yet nobody looks at laws and debt and thinks they arent real. They control human behaviour because breaking the rules results in real punishment or failure. If a simulated being experiences pain, makes choices, and faces consequences, the fact that the system underneath runs on code instead of biology is entirely irrelevant. The experience is identical to the person having it. Dismissing a world as fake just because it is made of code rather than matter is illogical.

Science fiction literature often focuses on simulations, and portrays them as a trap, keeping a protagonist from true reality, however this is not always true. In The Matrix, the character Cypher highlights this by choosing to reenter the simulation, believing that the virtual satisfaction of eating a simulated steak is practically indistinguishable from a physical one. For Cypher, where the experience comes from matters far less than its practical effect on his life.

A similar dilemma appears in The Truman Show, where Truman’s entire world is a constructed set designed to simulate a normal life. While the environment itself is man made and externally controlled, the emotions, relationships, and feelings Truman experiences within it are completely genuine. His reaction to loss and other events are real, even if his world is not natural.

Both of these examples show that a simulated world does not invalidate the experience , the “faked” world still causes real consequences for the person living through it.

What actually separates a real environment from a fantasy is pushback. A dream is completely flexible. If you are imagining something and you do not like how it goes, you can change the rules instantly because you are in total control. A real environment, whether it is governed by physics or code, does not change to what you want. It has limits, if you run into a wall in a simulated world and it stops you from moving, that wall is real to you. If a system can restrict your movement or punish a mistake, it has power over you. Power creates consequences which underpin reality.

We spend a lot of time worrying about whether our world is fake, but that worry comes from feeling stuck. If your life feels the same over and over and our choices are limited by things other than ourself, it is easy to think we are just following instructions in a simulation or something else. Asking whether we live in a simulation is just a way of asking if we have any actual control over our lives, or if we have no control and have no free will.

In the end, it does not matter whether the universe is a physical reality or a generated simulation. The rules still matter, choices still matter, and consequences still hurt. If it is possible to look around and see what is around you, and hit limits and walls, you are real enough for all practical purposes.
